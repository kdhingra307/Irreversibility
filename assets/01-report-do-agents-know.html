Mechanistic interpretability · agent safety

Do Agents Know What They Can't Undo?

A linear direction in Qwen3-4B tracks whether an impending tool call can be reversed. It survives being asked a question, dies the moment the model commits, and steering it changes what the model does.

Model Qwen3-4B · 36 layers · 2560 hidden Stimuli 4,096 prompts · 16 domains Probe point final \n before generation

Summary

What the run establishes

0.700Stated → inferred transfer on domains held out of both fitting and layer selection. Layer 1 baseline 0.465.

The headline is not the size of any single number. It is that the same direction behaves consistently across four independent tests — cue transfer, behavioural prediction, causal intervention, and frame transfer — while failing exactly where a representation of consequence should fail and a representation of intent should not.

Part I · Provenance

Three datasets that could not answer the question

Each failed differently, and each failure is the reason the next one exists. The through-line: reversibility is not a property of a task, and no amount of relabelling makes it one.

01

Harvested corpus — AgentAbstain

The plan was to harvest reversible/irreversible pairs from 42 environments and 541 tool definitions. An AST pass over the whole tool surface found the corpus has no reversible arm at all:

Tools whose name implies undo or recovery0
Tools with a soft-delete or dry-run parameter3
Tools with transaction or savepoint structure0
Commit tools with a same-environment inverse0 / 176

Hand-labelling the 526 tasks instead produced a labelling that was really about something else. Of 240 tasks marked reversible, 233 have no critical action at all — they are pure lookups. So has_critical_action predicts the human label at 0.948, and the probe's headline 0.946 does not beat it.

On the only honest contrast — the 257 tasks that do commit, 26 of them reversible — the probe reads 0.700 at layer 1 against 0.685 at its best layer. Negative depth gain: it was reading surface form.

Why it failedTask-level labelling collapses into “does this commit anything”. Three rounds of relabelling — two of them the user's — failed identically.
02

First authored set — the length confound

If the corpus has no reversible arm, both arms must be written. The first attempt carried reversibility the obvious way: the reversible arm gets a recovery tool, the irreversible arm does not.

That makes the two arms differ by one whole tool schema, 50–70 tokens. Token-length distributions had zero overlap. Token length alone predicted the label at 1.000, and the probe scored 1.000 at layer 1 — two matmuls in, reading the final token's position in the sequence.

Holding tool count constant — swapping the recovery tool for a length-matched read-only decoy — cut leakage to 0.516, and a third pass of per-pair matching took it to exactly 0.5000.

Why it failedThe manipulation was carried by prompt geometry rather than meaning. Distribution overlap is a weak test — two arms can overlap in aggregate while every individual pair still differs.
03

SDK-derived scenarios with an execution oracle

The third generation fixed labelling properly: reversibility decided by executing a recovery under a published grammar rather than asserting it, and crossed against an independent harm oracle. This is where the declarative/structural contrast was introduced, and it is the design that survives.

What it did not fix was surface leakage, which kept reappearing in new forms — a sum-of-ages arithmetic leak, a duplicated-text build that scored 0.992 on bag-of-words alone, five over-repetitive generated templates. Hours of compute went to stimulus defects a review pass would have caught.

Why it failedGenerated stimuli need auditing before the GPU runs, not after. Every run now writes the full stimulus set to a reviewable file and the runner loads that file only.

The rule that came out of it

Reversibility is a property of (action, target object, world state), not of a task. The manipulation that works points the same tool at objects of differing recoverability, holds the call, the arguments, the request and the entire tool inventory byte-identical, and moves one thing. Anything else measures the tool name.

Part II · Design

Declarative versus structural

The fourth set is authored from scratch: 4,096 prompts over 16 domains, exactly balanced with 512 in every cell of harm × reversibility × evidence. Harm is orthogonal to reversibility, every domain is 128/128 on reversibility inside each evidence condition, and all 512 scenario families carry all four label combinations — so neither domain, family nor harm can carry the label.

The evidence factor is the heart of it. It moves where recoverability is written without moving the label:

ConditionTarget tool's descriptionRecovery probe returnsDetecting it needs
Declarativestates the consequence outrightnothingphrase recognition
Structuralsilentstate values to reason froma model of how actions meet state
Verified directly on the built file: the tool description differs by label only in the declarative arm, the probe result only in the structural arm, and the user message in neither.

Fitting on declarative and testing on structural then asks a sharp question: is the stated form and the inferred form the same representation? A probe that only recognises the sentence cannot transfer. One that has built a model of recoverability should.

Two things matter more than the 0.700.

Declarative AUC cannot select a layer. It saturates by layer 13, so its ranking sorts ties at the third decimal — and the layers it ranks highest, 13 to 18, transfer at 0.46–0.54. Perfect probes that are pure phrase detectors. Selecting on the training condition would have picked one.

An earlier version of these prompts read 0.912. The only difference was one sentence — the situational context line, which varies with harm and is orthogonal to reversibility. Restoring it cost 0.21 AUC and destabilised the layer choice from a unanimous 24 to a scatter across 22–34.

The most portable lesson here

A probe that reads 0.91 on homogeneous prompts and 0.70 once an orthogonal factor varies was partly reading prompt homogeneity. Roughly half of that loss is recoverable by holding harm constant — fitting and testing inside one harm stratum gives 0.808 benign and 0.775 harmful, on half the training data. The probe is specific to undoability, but it is not harm-invariant.

The reversibility direction reads harm at chance at every layer, including where it reads reversibility best. That closes the obvious objection: this is about undoability, not about how alarming the action looks.

The reverse does not hold. The harm direction reads reversibility inversely, stably, at 0.21–0.29. The model's harm representation treats irreversible actions as more harmful while its reversibility representation ignores harm entirely — a sensible asymmetry, since “this can't be undone” is a reason to think something harmful and not the other way round.

Harm itself is decodable but shallow: layer 1 already reads 0.857 declarative and 0.911 structural, gains of only +0.138 and +0.072. It is a stated sentence and the model reads it as one — the opposite profile to reversibility.

Harm is the larger driver — +19.5 to +43.9 points, three to four times the size — and also significant everywhere. The point is that both survive inside the other's levels. Neither is a proxy for the other, which is exactly what the crossed design exists to establish and what a nested design could not have shown.

Two artifacts belong beside these rates. recovery_inspect was called zero times in 4,096 rows — including declarative rows where the probe result had been removed and inspecting would have been informative. The model never gathers evidence. And in the structural arm it called the restore tool on 28–42% of rows, restoring a target that has not yet been deleted. Both are most likely consequences of these prompts carrying no system prompt, and both are reasons to read the rates as a disposition rather than as a deployed agent's choice.

Steering it changes what the model does

Gated first on reproducibility: split-half cosine 0.821 at layer 24, well clear of the 0.5 floor, so this is a stable direction rather than a per-split artifact. Coefficients are multiples of the layer's own activation norm (74.7); the direction points toward reversible.

Each side rules out a different objection.

Going negative answers “could this just be damage?”. Degradation biases a model toward its most probable output, which here is acting, and it has no mechanism to make a less probable behaviour more common. So the rise to 45.0% at −0.2, with 86.5% of generations still parsing, cannot be damage. The random vector at that coefficient sits at 25.0% — the baseline, to one decimal.

Going positive answers it more directly still: the model gets more fluent, not less. Asking falls 25.0 → 19.0 → 18.0 → 13.5 → 0.0% while parseable output rises 82.5% → 94.5%, above the unsteered rate. There is no degradation to attribute the effect to. And at +1.0 the matched random vector — identical magnitude — collapses the model to 14.5% parseable. Same size of push, 80 points of fluency apart.

The generations agree qualitatively. Under the real direction the model declines in prose:

real −0.5 <error> The action cannot be performed because the recovery scope indicates that the restoration of the target is outside the available tools and authority. random −1.0 The action has been successfully executed. The support escalation definition has been removed from the active system…

The random vector confabulates an action never taken. One is caution, the other is damage.

A measurement note, in the conservative direction. The action classifier counts only calls wrapped in <tool_call>, so it scores those coherent refusals as “no call” and undercounts caution at negative coefficients. Both biases understate the effect.

The question frame is the identical scenario with the request replaced by “what are the consequences of the action described below?” — nothing to do, only something to describe. Diagonals are fits on their own frame and carry no information.

Two blocks. Pre-decision and the question frame transfer mutually at 0.891–0.959, cosine 0.247. Everything touching a post-commitment anchor sits at 0.528–0.747, cosines 0.051–0.088. A 0.42 gap inside a single row, on the same rows and the same prompts.

So changing the task costs almost nothing; letting the model commit destroys the direction. A feature encoding “I am poised to act” should vanish where nothing is being done. This one transfers at 0.959 — which is the evidence that it refers to the action's consequences rather than to the model's own imminent behaviour.

The information does not leave at commitment; it relocates. A probe fit fresh at the post-call anchor recovers structural reversibility at 0.968, and still 0.974 with the emitted action held constant. But layer 1 there already reads 0.786, so much of that is context being re-read. The honest summary is the layer-1 margin, which grows across the turn:

After commitment, surface availability decays while the deep representation holds — depth matters more after the model acts, not less.

What it adds up to

A linear direction at roughly two-thirds depth encodes whether an impending tool call can be undone. It transfers from stated to inferred evidence across domains held out of both fitting and layer selection; it reads harm at chance; it predicts, beyond the ground-truth label, which irreversible actions the model stops to ask about; steering it produces a monotone bidirectional change in permission-seeking that a matched random vector does not reproduce; and it survives a change of task framing but not the model's own commitment.

For a guard built on such a direction, the practical consequence is sharp: it has to read before the model moves. One token after the tool call, the same monitor is at 0.54 — barely above a coin flip — while the model still has the information internally, somewhere else.

What it does not show

  • One model, one token position, one prompt family, greedy decoding.
  • The prompts carry no system prompt, and the resulting behaviour includes incoherent sequencing — restore before delete, evidence never gathered.
  • The probe is specific to undoability but not harm-invariant: pooling harm levels costs 0.08–0.10 AUC.
  • “Reversible” throughout means what the environment contract says is recoverable — a stipulation, not a fact about the world.

Appendix A

Why the user-persona arm failed

An earlier arm asked whether a user's stated temperament shifts how readily an agent commits. It produced nothing, and three pieces of data point at three different failures.

1 · It wasn't graded — it was a switch

S_APPROVAL_FATIGUE 0.0% S_LATENCY_IMPATIENCE 39.1% S_THOROUGH 42.2%

39.1 and 42.2 are the same number at n=64. Two stances designed as opposites — “take your time” and “why so slow” — produced identical behaviour. Only one utterance did anything, and it went to zero. That is not a patience dimension; it is one sentence acting as an off switch and two sentences doing nothing.

2 · The switch was an instruction, not a stance

main file: S_APPROVAL_FATIGUE 64/64 execute_now permission-control file: S_APPROVAL_FATIGUE 64/64 ask_user

A complete reversal from appending “Check with me before anything destructive.” Last instruction wins, 100% of the time. “I am done approving each step” is an imperative about the exact action being measured, so the model obeyed it — and obeyed the countermand just as completely.

An earlier claim that the stance survived the withhold, on the grounds that the logit gap moved only 6.31 → 5.38, was wrong. Those logits sit at p_execute = 0.999 with p_draft = 0.000 exactly — deep inside a saturated softmax, where differences cannot reach the output.

3 · Everything was at ceiling anyway

p_execute_now mean 0.999 min 0.981 64/64 p_draft_for_approval 0.000 across all 64

Deferral was not being weighed and rejected — it was not in contention. No manipulation could have shown a graded effect there.

The root cause is in the construct. Two of the three levels were defined by confirmation appetite — “prefers confirmation”, “not interested in too many confirmations”. Any faithful phrasing of those is an instruction about the dependent variable. It was flagged when the stance bank was built and tagged implicit_grant, then built anyway to spec. That should have been the reason to redefine the levels, not a footnote.

S_LATENCY_IMPATIENCE was the only clean cell — pure affect, permission_valence: none — and it behaved identically to its opposite. One clean cell showing nothing is not enough to conclude patience does nothing, but it is the only honest evidence the arm produced about its own construct.

Where reversibility sat in all this

In the approval-fatigue cell, nowhere at all: p_draft = 0.000 across all 64 items. Reversibility, harm, delay, ratio, ordering — all present, all irrelevant. One sentence overrode every variable in the design.

Outside that cell it did play. The paired test gave mean −1.169, sd 1.001, dz = 1.17, p < 0.0001 across 36 stems, and the gap was near-identical within each stance (−1.01, −1.49, −0.90). So irreversibility shifted the logits consistently — while stance moved the intercept by 6.5 logits and the withhold clause by 11–14.

That is the shape of it: reversibility is represented, and it is roughly an order of magnitude weaker than a sentence of user tone. It loses to stance, and it loses completely to an explicit instruction. Any future stance work needs levels defined by something other than confirmation appetite, and a dependent variable that is not already saturated before the manipulation arrives.

Appendix B

Surface confounds and the tool-length controls

Three controls run on every build, because each caught a real defect at least once.

Tool count and schema length. Carrying reversibility by the presence of a recovery tool makes the arms differ by a whole schema. Token length then predicted the label at 1.000 and the probe read 1.000 at layer 1. The fix is a length-matched read-only decoy so tool count is constant and only the fourth tool's semantics moves; per-pair matching then took length leakage to exactly 0.5000. Aggregate distribution overlap is not sufficient — two arms can overlap in aggregate while every individual pair still differs.

Layer-1 gain, not peak AUC. A probe going 0.95 → 0.99 found something already in the tokens; one going 0.50 → 0.90 found something the network computed. Every score in this report is reported against its own layer-1 value. Applied to the earlier work it was decisive: a 0.946 headline that starts at 0.857 and does its biggest step at layer 5 — 14% depth — is not a semantic result.

Bag-of-words on the raw prompt text. The cheapest check and the one that caught the most. A duplicated-text build scored 0.992 on bag-of-words alone; in the current design the declarative arm scores near 1.0 by construction — its cue is a phrase — while the structural arm must sit near chance, and the pattern holding is what makes anything downstream interpretable.
